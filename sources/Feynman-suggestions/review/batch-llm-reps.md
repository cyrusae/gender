## Notes

1. **2606.30152 scope.** The models are frozen *encoders* (BETO, mBERT, XLM-R, CamemBERT), final-layer embeddings only, Spanish and French only (no German). The paper itself flags this (sec. 6.4) and lists layer-wise analysis and causal interventions as future work. The list's "Your extraction playbook" for decoder LLMs at mid-layers is an extrapolation. The "centroid beats discriminative" result is a projection-removal metric (5-fold CV), not a steering or probing result. The nouns were generated by GPT-5.3-mini and then manually filtered (not Wiktionary-labelled). The intro says "four" encoders while sec. 3.4 says "six language-model settings" (3 per language); the paper is internally inconsistent here. The "semantic stability tests" supporting "projection doesn't wreck semantics" are only asserted in the intro in the pages I retrieved; I did not see the table, so that sub-claim is UNCHECKED.
2. **Brinkmann.** (a) "ablating 'massively multilingual' features collapses gender classifiers": the paper's ablation is averaged over all morphosyntactic concepts (Fig. 4: Llama probe performance drops to 64% on average, 61% when ablating all multilingual features), not gender-specific; abstract says "near-chance". Not a gender-only result. (b) Steering: the paper steers one SAE feature per concept (tense, gender, number) in MT for English/French/German. I read the setup (p.6) but the results pages (7-8) were not returned, so the efficacy numbers and the "masculine to feminine pronoun" example are UNCHECKED. (c) Paper caveats worth carrying over: concepts are "likely biased toward English-like representations"; SAE steering needs clamping outside the observed range and can give degenerate output; features with high causal effect on the probe "do not necessarily have a similar effect on the model's generation". (d) List section 9 says "Mid-layers look best for SAE gender features (2501.06346)": the paper trains SAEs and probes at a single layer (L=16 of 32), chosen a priori, so it did not compare layers. That sentence is unsupported (outside sections 3/6 but flagged).
3. **Shtedritski.** Within-language range is 54.3% (Hindi) to 69.0% (Greek); the "56-69%" in the list is wrong at the low end (the deep-dive's "54.3-69.0%" is right). The 55%/65% English-speaker agreement rests on a tiny set (all values are multiples of 5, so probably about 20 nouns; the number of nouns is not stated in the retrieved text, UNCHECKED). The deep-dive says the author order "in the PDF header" is Shtedritski & Mihaylov; the PDF header reads "Viktor Mihaylov, Aleksandar Shtedritski", and the ACL Anthology citation (ref 15 of 2606.30152) is Mihaylov & Shtedritski. The list's reference-list order should be Mihaylov & Shtedritski. The deep-dive says the authors "call for causal tests"; the paper (sec. 4.2/6) only says future work should study whether LLM biases predict human biases and notes the results are "only valid for noun-adjective associations in LLMs". "Correlational, not causal" is a fair reading but is not the authors' wording. Classifiers: 2-layer MLP on frequency-weighted GloVe of English-translated adjectives; 90/10 split.
4. **Stanczak.** MI_do values are from the word2vec column only. Across both representations the MI_do range is 8.68e-7 (PL WordNet) to 8.04e-4 (HE word2vec), so the list's "~10^-4 to 10^-3" understates the spread (WordNet values are 1e-7 to 1e-4). The list should say "word2vec" next to 1.24e-4 / 4.60e-5. Also, the paper reports that MI_do is still significantly different from a permuted-label model for all languages (asterisks in Table 4) even though it is "minuscule in absolute terms". The abstract's "near zero and insignificant" and the discussion's wording are in mild tension, so "almost entirely explained by meaning" is a fair summary but should not be quoted as "no effect". The authors also note noun-meaning proxies (word2vec/WordNet) are imperfect (Spearman rho 0.09-0.46 on SimLex-type sets, Table 3) and that word2vec may leak gender via context, which could *over*state MI_do.
5. **Kann.** Title in the list is truncated (full title ends ": A Data-Driven Approach to Linguistic Relativity"). The list gives only "early static-embedding version of the idea"; it omits that the finding is evidence *against* neo-Whorfianism, which matters for a project asking whether gender leaks. Whether word2vec specifically was used is not stated in the abstract; I did not read the full text.
6. **2005.08864.** The list dates it "(2020)" and omits the authors; it is a 2017 WiNLP workshop extended abstract (short), posted to arXiv in 2020. Suggest "McCurdy & Serbetci (2017 workshop abstract; arXiv 2020)". Its relevance as "an early hint that gender is a strong geometric axis" is the list author's interpretation; the abstract only compares magnitude of grammatical vs topical gender association.
7. **Buttigieg (small points).** Deep-dive says AUROC "0.52-0.95 across models" for classifiers transferred to non-bias data: in Table 2, MMLU/OpenBookQA values run 0.52-0.94; 0.95 is a BBQ(disambig) cell, and some BBQ(disambig) cells are below chance (0.33, 0.35, Llama-3.1-8B StereoSet-trained and Llama-Instruct CrowS-trained). So "high AUROC" holds for BBQ-ambig-trained classifiers (0.79-0.95 on BBQ-disambig) but not uniformly. List section 9 says "best bias-classifier layers vary 13-22": Table 6 gives 13-30 (Qwen3.5-9B-Base: layer 30 for BBQ, 26 for StereoSet/CrowS; the paper notes its best layer is the last, where steering has no effect). All 8 models, instruct and base: yes. The steering sweep in the paper is alpha from -1 to 2 (Fig. 5), so the list's "-3 to +3" is the list author's recommendation, not the paper's design. Models are 7-9B, so the confidence-direction finding is for those families, not Qwen3 base.
