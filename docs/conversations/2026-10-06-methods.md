> Could you give me a more targeted overview of the tech/methodology involved in the kind of work we’re looking at for the verbs of motion and gender LLM studies? I have an abstract conceptual understanding of what probing a model means but not a real or actionable one.

Here's the roadmap, then the first piece. Your two studies sit at different levels, which shapes the toolkit for each:

1. **What a "representation" physically is**, and how you extract it. This covers both studies.
2. **Probing**: training a small classifier on those representations, plus the controls that keep it honest.
3. **Causal methods**: concept erasure and steering, which test whether the model actually *uses* what the probe found. This is the core of the gender study.
4. **Behavioral methods**: log-probabilities and prompted ratings, where the motion-verb valence replication mostly lives. Then I'll cover where probing could extend it.

**Piece 1: what you're actually probing**

A transformer processes text one token at a time. After each layer, every token is represented by a vector of numbers, for example 4,096 floats in an 8B model. This running vector is called the **residual stream**. Each layer reads from it and adds back to it. "The model's representation of *mesa* at layer 14" literally means that 4,096-number vector at that token position after layer 14.

To get at those vectors, you use a library that hooks into the forward pass. **TransformerLens** and **nnsight** are the standard ones. The workflow looks like this:

- Load a model, such as Llama, Gemma, or Qwen. Small ones like Gemma-2-2B run on a laptop.
- Feed it a sentence like *"La mesa es grande."*
- Ask the library to cache the activations, which gives you a tensor shaped roughly [layers × tokens × hidden_dim].
- Pull out the vector for the token you care about, at every layer.

Do that for a few thousand nouns and you have a dataset with one row per noun and 4,096 columns, labeled with whatever you know about each noun (masculine/feminine, animate/inanimate, and so on).

Here's a concrete way to think about it from linguistics. Each vector is like a feature bundle in phonology, except the features are unlabeled and there are thousands of them. Probing asks whether some property you care about, like [+feminine], can be read off that bundle.

**Piece 2: probing mechanics and controls**

*The mechanics are almost boring:*
- Take your noun vectors at one layer and train a **logistic regression** on them, with masculine/feminine labels. scikit-learn handles this in about ten lines.
- Repeat for every layer and plot test accuracy by layer.
- The shape of that curve is usually the actual finding: where a property appears, peaks, and fades.

*The controls, mapped to your confounds:*

1. **Probe capacity.** With 4,096 dimensions and a few thousand examples, a classifier can fit almost anything. Hewitt & Liang (2019) introduced **control tasks** for this. You give each noun a *random but consistent* label and train the same probe. If it still scores well, the probe is memorizing words rather than reading a property. You report **selectivity**, which is real accuracy minus control accuracy, and you stick to linear probes.

2. **Spelling.** Build the dataset so ending doesn't predict gender:
   - Exceptions like *el mapa, el día, la mano, la foto*.
   - Opaque endings like *la calle, el puente, la flor, el árbol*.
   - A balanced set where an "-a means feminine" heuristic scores at chance.

   German is a natural contrast here, since its gender is far less form-predictable.

3. **Agreement leakage.** In Spanish the determiner comes before the noun, so the *mesa* token has already seen *la*. Some ways around that:
   - Use frames with gender-invariant determiners: *mis mesas, sus puentes*.
   - Use bare nouns in a list context.
   - Compare the two conditions, which is itself a finding.

4. **Memorization vs. abstraction.** This is your last point, operationalized:
   - Split train and test **by lexeme**, never by sentence.
   - Test on **nonce nouns**. Pseudowords with ambiguous endings, such as *el/la brunde*, show whether the probe reads a lexical gender feature or just form.

5. **Tokenization.** Many nouns split into several subword tokens. Decide up front whether you read the last token or average over all of them, and keep that choice consistent.

Yes, though "injection" covers two moves with opposite logic, and you'll want both. You also need something to inject first.

**Piece 3: causal methods**

*Step 0: get two directions, not one.* Your probe gives you a grammatical-gender direction. You need a **separate social-gender direction**, built from contexts where the referent's sex matters. The simplest version is a **difference of means**: average the vectors of feminine examples, subtract the average of masculine examples, and you get a direction. It's often cleaner than the probe's weights.

This is where your linguistics earns its keep. Spanish person nouns like *el médico / la médica* tie grammatical and social gender together, so they can't separate the two. **Epicenes** can: *la persona, la víctima, el bebé, la criatura*. Grammatical gender stays fixed while the referent's social gender varies freely. Inanimates (*la mesa, el puente*) give you grammatical gender with no social gender at all.

*Step 1: geometry, which is cheap and suggestive.* Compute the cosine similarity between the two directions, layer by layer. High overlap is a hint, not proof, because directions can align without the model *using* the alignment.

*Step 2: the two kinds of intervention.*

1. **Erasure (subtraction).** Remove the grammatical-gender direction from the residual stream. **LEACE** (Belrose et al., 2023) is the standard method; it provably removes all *linearly* available information about a concept. Then measure social-gender behavior. If social-gender judgments degrade, the two share machinery. If they're untouched, the two are separable.

2. **Steering (addition).** This is your injection. Add the grammatical-gender direction, scaled, while the model processes something, and watch whether social-gender outputs move. An example: push a feminine grammatical vector into an epicene context, then see whether the model's pronoun choices or attribute descriptions for that person shift.

The **Boroditsky bridge** becomes your behavioral readout here. Inject while the model processes an inanimate noun, then measure the log-probabilities of stereotyped adjectives: *elegant/beautiful* vs. *strong/sturdy*. The human finding is that German speakers, for whom *Brücke* is feminine, describe bridges more stereotypically femininely than Spanish speakers, for whom *puente* is masculine. Steering lets you ask whether the model shows the effect *and* whether grammatical gender causes it.

A third tool, **activation patching**, swaps a vector from one run into another, for example the vector for *la persona* into the *el bebé* sentence. It's useful for localizing *where* the effect lives, but it's secondary for your question.

Before piece 4, here's a design question. Say steering with the grammatical-gender direction *does* shift the adjective probabilities. What dose-response pattern would convince you it's a real effect rather than you just shoving the model off-distribution until it outputs noise? What would you run alongside it as a control?

Your instinct is right. The mutual exclusivity is a fact about the *labels*, not about the *geometry*. Two features can be mutually exclusive in the data without being opposite ends of one axis. The good news is you don't have to pick: it's an empirical question you can test, and the test is interesting in its own right.

**Why the binary setup can't answer it**

If you train on masculine vs. feminine only, you get one axis by construction. Difference of means and logistic regression both just return the direction separating the two classes. You can't recover two features from two classes, because you have nothing to measure each of them *from*.

**What you need: a baseline class**

With a gender-neutral reference point, you can compute two directions:

- **masc = masculine mean − neutral mean**
- **fem = feminine mean − neutral mean**

Then compare them:

- **Cosine near −1:** they're antiparallel, so it really is one axis.
- **Cosine near 0, or not reliably negative:** you have two separate features that happen never to co-occur.
- **Very different lengths:** one class sits close to the baseline and the other is far away. That would be the most interesting result.

**Where the baseline comes from**

- **German neuter.** This is worth reconsidering, since you'd excluded it. As a *target* class it complicates the bleed-through question, but as a *reference point* it's exactly what this test needs. That's a strong argument for bringing it back in that role.
- **Spanish.** There's no neuter noun class, so you'd improvise. Options include gender-invariant adjectives (*grande, feliz*), epicenes with unspecified referents, or a cross-lingual anchor.
- **Social gender.** Unspecified referents (*someone, la persona* with no further cues) can serve as the neutral class.

**Dose-response: the setup**

Steering is one line of math, applied at one layer:

> **h′ = h + α · v**

- **h** is the residual-stream vector at the token you're steering.
- **v** is your grammatical-gender direction, normalized to length 1.
- **α** is the dose. It's typically scaled relative to the typical norm of h at that layer, because raw numbers are meaningless across layers.

You sweep α across a range, both negative and positive, and record two things at each dose:

1. **The target metric.** For the bridge setup, that's the log-prob of *elegant/beautiful* minus the log-prob of *strong/sturdy*.
2. **A damage metric.** That's how far you've broken the model: the KL divergence between steered and unsteered outputs on unrelated text, or perplexity on a neutral sentence.

You then plot both curves against α, and the relationship between them is where the evidence lives.

**The damage metric**

The idea is to measure how much steering changes the model's behavior on things it *shouldn't* affect.

**KL divergence** is the standard choice:

1. Take a set of neutral prompts unrelated to gender, such as ordinary Spanish sentences from Wikipedia.
2. Run each prompt twice, once clean and once with your steering applied at the same layer and the same α.
3. At each position, compare the two next-token probability distributions using KL divergence, which measures how different two probability distributions are.
4. Average over prompts and positions. Zero means no damage, and it climbs as you break things.

**Loss on held-out text** is a simpler alternative: does steering make the model worse at predicting normal text? It's correlated with KL and easier to explain.

There's no universal threshold for how much is "too much." You calibrate it with the first control below. Steer with **random directions of the same norm**, and their damage curve tells you how much harm *any* push of that size causes. Your working window is the α range where your direction's damage stays near, or below, the random baseline.

**One design choice matters a lot:** steer **only the target-noun positions**, not every token. Steering everything inflates damage and blurs where the effect is coming from.

Your reading of the graph is right, too: (b) is the pattern where the target metric only moves once damage spikes. Signal is the target moving while damage stays flat.

**Controls that separate (a) from (c)**

1. **Random directions**, the same ones as above. They also give you a null distribution for the target metric: how much do the adjectives move under arbitrary pushes?
2. **Off-target adjective pairs.** If the model just gets "flowery," non-gendered pairs move too. Use pairs that differ on other dimensions (*old/new, big/small, ornate/plain*) and match them on frequency and valence. For norming, the **Glasgow Norms** include human gender-association ratings for about 5,500 English words. That gives you a graded predictor instead of hand-picked pairs, so you can test whether the shift scales with how gendered each adjective is rated.
3. **A different grammatical direction.** Steer with singular/plural at the same norm. If number also shifts your adjective metric, you're seeing "grammatical perturbation" in general, not gender.
4. **A positive control.** At the same doses, your direction should flip grammatical agreement: determiner and adjective forms in Spanish continuations. If agreement *doesn't* move but stereotyped adjectives do, something's off with what v actually encodes.
5. **Convergent erasure.** LEACE-remove the direction without steering and check that the baseline bridge-type asymmetry *shrinks*. Two methods pointing the same way is much stronger than either alone.

Control 3 assumes your direction itself is clean, and it might not be. Feminine Spanish nouns skew toward abstractions like *-ción* and *-dad*, so a naive direction can partly encode "abstract." The fix comes back to piece 2: match your masculine and feminine noun sets on semantic category and frequency before computing v.

> "At the same doses, your direction should flip grammatical agreement: determiner and adjective forms in Spanish continuations. If agreement doesn't move but stereotyped adjectives do, something's off with what v actually encodes." Say more?

The core idea: the most direct test of what a direction *is* is checking that it does its own job before you ask it to do anything else.

**Why agreement is the right test**

Your direction v was built from grammatical-gender labels. Grammatical gender exists to drive **agreement**. Its whole function is making *blanco/blanca*, *lo/la*, *el/la* match the noun. So if v really is the model's grammatical-gender feature, adding it at the noun should change what agrees with that noun downstream.

Concretely, steer *mesas* toward masculine in a frame like:

> *Compré mis mesas ___*

Then measure **P(blancas) − P(blancos)**.

This is about as clean a minimal pair as you'll get. It's the same adjective lemma and the same meaning, differing only in the agreement suffix, so semantics is held constant. Two details matter:

- Use the gender-invariant determiner frame from piece 2, so the model can't simply read gender off a preceding *la*.
- Use adjectives that inflect for gender, not invariant ones like *grande*.

**The 2×2 table: four outcomes and what each means**

| | **Adjectives shift** | **Adjectives don't shift** |
|---|---|---|
| **Agreement flips** | v is the grammatical-gender feature, *and* it influences stereotyped associations. That's your bleed-through evidence. | v is the feature, but there's no bleed-through at this layer. This is a clean, interpretable null. |
| **Agreement doesn't flip** | The suspicious cell (see below). | The dose is too small or the layer is wrong. Go back. |

**The suspicious cell**

Your probe found a direction that *correlates* with gender labels, but the model doesn't use it for its core grammatical job. Something else is moving the adjectives. Candidates:

- **The confounds from earlier:** semantic class (the *-ción/-dad* abstractness skew), word form, or general affect.
- **Redundancy:** the model may encode gender in several places. If you steer one copy, later layers can re-derive gender from the noun's identity and restore agreement. This is called **self-repair**, and it's a real, documented phenomenon. The diagnostic is to check whether *erasing* v at that layer breaks agreement. If erasure doesn't hurt agreement either, v isn't on the agreement pathway.

Either way, you can't call the adjective shift *grammatical gender bleeding into social gender*. All you can say is that *something* in v does.

**A bonus: it calibrates your dose**

The α at which agreement flips gives you a natural unit: the dose needed to change gender's primary function. If stereotyped adjectives shift at around that dose, that's coherent. If they only shift at many times that dose, deep into the KL-damage region, you're likely looking at scenario (b) wearing a costume.

This also gives a natural stopping point. If you want, piece 4 (behavioral methods and the motion-verb study) is next, or we can come back to which control to prototype first.

Control (4) fits that best:

A sensible prototype order:

1. **Build v and probe it with selectivity**, using the matched, confound-controlled noun set from piece 2.
2. **Gate: the agreement flip plus random directions.** If this fails, stop and fix the direction.
3. **Specificity: off-target adjectives (2) and the number direction (3).** These cost more because of the stimulus design work.
4. **Convergence: LEACE erasure (5).** This is the last step because it's the most setup, and it only matters once there's an effect to corroborate.

That ordering is really the whole methodology in miniature: validate the instrument, then measure, then rule out alternatives, then corroborate.
